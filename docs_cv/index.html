Abstract
Probing frozen vision transformers typically uses permutation-invariant aggregation (GAP or $\texttt{[CLS]}$), treating patch tokens as an unstructured set. Content-dependent probes such as self-attention are useful accuracy controls, but they do not expose a fixed token schedule or fixed position weights for auditing. We introduce SSMProbe, a probe of frozen vision transformers that learns a Sinkhorn patch route and reads it with a diagonal S4 decoder. The decoder's causal linear time-invariant (LTI) recurrence yields fixed position coefficients, exposing how patch arrangement interacts with sequential weighting. Held-out spatial predictability measures route organization beyond a location-bias null. On CUB and Cars, MAE, BEiT, DINOv2, and supervised ViT yield spatially organized routes with distinct readout profiles: DINOv2 is $\texttt{[CLS]}$-dominant, while MAE benefits from learned routing over fixed scans. On MAE/ImageNet, Sinkhorn reaches 70.33% accuracy, 5.55 percentage points above the strongest fixed scan.

The Physics Is the Network: Fast, Training-Free Neural Emulation
Authors: Luka Todorovic, Kaushal Gianchandani, Sai Ravela
First: 2026-10-14T17:24:48+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-14T17:24:48+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Neural emulation need not require learning a substitute for known physics. We investigate PolyNets that couple a continuous-time neural representation of polynomial dynamics with a simple recurrent network for discrete-time integration. When the governing coefficients are known, the resulting tensor graph requires no training and preserves the prescribed numerical scheme while enabling efficient hardware execution and parallel simulation. Using the one-dimensional Korteweg-de Vries equation, we compare PolyNet with CPU and GPU finite-difference Runge-Kutta solvers and three trained neural models: a state-map emulator, a physics-informed predictor, and a learned-tendency integrator. Analytical soliton solutions provide a reference for amplitude and phase errors across grid resolutions, 10^6 dispersively scaled time steps, ensembles of up to 10^4 trajectories, and varying initial amplitudes. PolyNet achieves accuracy comparable to the numerical solvers, including at amplitudes outside the learned models' training conditions. It combines this fidelity with high GPU throughput, approximately constant over the tested resolution range, and favorable ensemble scaling.

The COUNT: Counting N-grams Unlocks Scaling on Host Memory and Training-Free Adaptation
Authors: Ian Magnusson, Noah A. Smith, Pang Wei Koh
First: 2026-10-14T16:38:19+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-14T16:38:19+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
The scaling relationship of compute and performance for language models (LMs) has been extensively studied; more recently, models are being designed to scale memory use, as well. In particular, adding embeddings for n-grams scales memory separately from compute use, because each pass only processes individual embedding rows, which are much smaller than the storage used for the whole embedding table. However, existing methods train embeddings from scratch, which needs expensive GPU memory for fast reading and writing. Meanwhile, the ordinary memory of the host system is cheap and underutilized by these training methods. To scale on host memory, we introduce COUNT (Counting Offline Unlocks N-gram Transformers), which builds n-gram embeddings without adding learned parameters by storing precomputed n-gram counts on host. We use the corpus frequencies to weight together the LM's own unembeddings of the next tokens most often following the current n-gram history. At a fixed compute budget, COUNT can be added as an additional input embedding for pretraining 1B-parameter LMs to improve validation loss as much as an LM with 57% more compute. If GPU memory is limited, using COUNT to let backbone parameters fill GPU memory gives better performance than splitting memory with additional learned n-gram embeddings. Beyond efficiency, COUNT allows switching in a different table of n-gram counts from a domain-specific corpus at inference time to adapt to a new task without any training.

Abstract
Human demonstrations are today used largely for pretraining vision-language-action models, with little ability to use the same data for target-embodiment or task-oriented training, which scalable deployment of robots in industrial and general environments depends on. A human demonstration recorded on a simple and scalable device such as a mobile phone can serve as post-training data for a robot whose body differs in structure and capability from the human who recorded it. This is made possible not by retargeting the hand's motion but the object's. A capture yields a body-free record: the object's pose over time, its contact intervals, and a reconstructed 3D object with provenance. Any robot, given as a URDF and a short descriptor, is solved against that record and scored in simulation against the demonstrated object motion as ground truth: the human-to-simulation leg of human-to-simulation-to-robot transfer, measured per robot. Each (robot, episode) pair gets one outcome: pass, failure with a named mode, or refusal, with its reason, before any motion. The method runs as a working system over a growing collection; the set analysed here is a sample of it: 42 captures solved on 8 robots, 157 pairs, of which 31.2% pass, 52.2% fail, 14.0% are refused and 2.5% are unauditable (kinematic feasibility, +/-50 mm / +/-15 deg, in simulation). Refusals held up: every pair the checks refuse on stored evidence yet ran anyway failed (13 of 13; 5 on a body whose public model welds its hand). A description file could not be shown to screen a robot: its outcomes were barely more predictable than guessing. No policy has yet been trained on the record. The record decides what a robot may post-train on; whether the policy then works on the real robot is the simulation-to-robot question, answered downstream.

Abstract

Abstract
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have made substantial progress in spatial reasoning, and self-evolving agents further extend these capabilities to embodied environments by learning from interactions. However, current self-evolution remains particularly challenging in embodied spatial tasks, complex and multi-step decisions from partial visual evidence making it difficult to determine why an execution fails. The failure may stem from misperception, insufficient exploration, or incorrect planning, yet execution feedback alone rarely reveals which component is responsible. As a result, simply accumulating interaction experience can reinforce ineffective strategies. To address this, we propose HiMeco, a brain-inspired self-evolving agent for embodied spatial intelligence, inspired by metacognitive monitoring and hippocampal memory consolidation. Through Hippocampal-Metacognitive Reflection, HiMeco diagnoses failures in perception-exploration-planning (PEP), then compares execution attempts to derive reusable strategies and guidance for avoiding recurring errors. It also abstracts interaction programs into parameterized skills, using evidence contracts and execution-based validation to determine their retention and reuse. These strategies and skills guide subsequent interactions, with memory updated only between rounds and model parameters kept frozen. HiMeco achieves state-of-the-art performance on ESI-Bench, and it also improves accuracy on EmbSpatial-Bench by 3.94 percentage points without evolution.

Abstract
Vision-language models have shown strong performance, but they often generalize poorly to specialized domains. While semi-supervised vision-language learning mitigates this limitation by leveraging a small set of labeled image-text pairs together with abundant unlabeled images, existing methods remain fundamentally pairwise and fail to model the global structure of multimodal representation manifolds. Most existing topology-based alignment methods rely on persistence diagram matching, which neither guarantees geometric alignment nor uses the image-text pairing information central to vision-language learning. We propose Topology-Aware Representation Alignment for Semi-Supervised Vision-Language Learning (ToMA), a framework that uses persistent homology to decompose pairwise relations into $H_0$-death and $H_1$-birth relations and aligns them across modalities through available cross-modal correspondences. This lightweight decomposition captures both connectivity and cycle-closing structure without constructing 2-simplices. Experiments show clear improvements on remote sensing, while fashion results reveal strong gains in classification and particularly strong retrieval improvements for difficult queries. Additional analysis shows that ToMA provides more consistent gains than alternative topology-based objectives and that lightweight $H_1$-birth edges provide useful higher-order structural signals.

Abstract
While deep learning-based denoising has become widely adopted in low-dose CT, conventional models use generic architectures designed for natural images, failing to account for non-stationary and spatially correlated CT noise characteristics. Attention Transfer Is Not Universally Effective for Vision Transformers
Authors: Huaiyuan Qin, Muli Yang, Gabriel James Goenawan, Peng Hu, Chen Gong, Xi Peng, Hongyuan Zhu
Venue: NeurIPS 2026
First: 2026-05-08T03:39:38+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-14T13:35:30+00:00
Comments: Published in NeurIPS 2026
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
A recent work shows that Attention Transfer, which transfers only the attention patterns from a pre-trained teacher Vision Transformer (ViT) to a randomly initialized standard student ViT, is sufficient to recover the full benefit of the teacher's pre-trained weights. We revisit this finding on a comprehensive benchmark of 20 teachers from 11 well-known ViT families and reveal that Attention Transfer is not universally effective. While 7 families transfer successfully, 4 consistently fail, falling up to 5.1\% below the from-scratch no-transfer baseline. Further results demonstrate that this failure is family-consistent across model sizes, and persists under extended training durations, different transfer datasets, and out-of-distribution evaluations. Controlled analyses then consistently localize the problem to the attention-routing channel, indicating that the key issue is not whether the student can match the teacher's attention patterns, but whether the matched patterns remain functional for the student. Crucially, we identify architectural mismatch between the pre-trained teacher and the standard student as the primary mechanism. By adding only the teacher's native architectural components to the student in a randomly initialized state, we completely reverse the failure for all 4 families. Notably, these components alone do not improve from-scratch training, confirming that they specifically unlock the usability of the teacher's attention. We further show that this failure is not explained by the inadequate choice of transfer loss, differences in pre-training recipes, or the intrinsic quality of attention maps. Our findings refine the prevailing understanding of attention in ViT representations: attention is sufficient \textit{only} when the student architecture matches the teacher.

Capability $\neq$ Interpretability: Human Interpretability of Vision Foundation Models
Authors: Julien Colin, Lore Goetschalckx, Nuria Oliver, Thomas Serre
First: 2026-05-19T18:00:06+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-14T13:05:25+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
How interpretable are the features of leading vision models? The question is increasingly pressing as these models move from research benchmarks into high-stakes deployments, yet existing methods cannot answer it reliably. We address this gap with a framework for measuring and comparing the human interpretability of vision models, built around two complementary psychophysics protocols: (1) localizability---can an observer predict where a feature fires on a novel image?---and (2) semanticity---can an observer accurately describe what the feature represents? Features are recovered via sparse autoencoders, and a chance-anchored scoring function places every model on a common scale. Applying the framework to six vision transformers---two supervised ViTs and four foundation models (DINOv2, DINOv3, CLIP, SigLIP)---we collected more than $15{,}000$ behavioral responses, of which we analyzed $13{,}400$ responses from the $377$ participants who passed our pre-specified quality checks. We find that foundation models are consistently $less$ interpretable than their size-matched supervised counterparts, and the gap is not a capability tradeoff: interpretability does not correlate with downstream task performance on any benchmark we examine. What does correlate is the locality of a feature's activations and coarse-grained semantic alignment with humans---models with focal activations and representations that reflect the world's broad categorical structure tend to produce more interpretable features. The two protocols yield strongly correlated rankings and share the same predictors, establishing interpretability as an independent, measurable dimension of representation quality---and, surprisingly, one on which the foundation models we tested fall below their size-matched supervised baseline. Capability alone cannot close that gap, but locality and coarse-grained alignment might.

Abstract
Vision-language models (VLMs) are moving onto edge devices, but little is known about how much energy they draw on single-board computers with NPU or GPU accelerators, or where that energy goes. We measure total-system energy, latency, and thermal behavior of Qwen2-VL-2B-Instruct and its text-only sibling on a Raspberry Pi 5 with a Hailo-10H NPU and on a Jetson Orin Nano Super, on both CPU and accelerator, and separate the fixed energy of each request from the energy that grows with output length. Because the accelerators use different quantization and serving software, we compare deployment stacks rather than silicon. Three findings stand out. First, NPU offloading makes the Pi a much more efficient VLM platform: it cuts energy per generated token by about two-thirds, leaves the host CPU largely free, and avoids the thermal throttling that the CPU backend reaches in sustained use. Second, an image adds both fixed and per-token energy, but at typical response lengths generating the answer costs far more than processing the image. Third, the Jetson GPU favors short responses through its lower fixed cost per request, whereas the Pi NPU favors longer ones through its lower cost per output token; their energy curves cross near 40 output tokens.

Abstract
Autoregressive transformers trained on limit order book (LOB) message streams are increasingly proposed as foundation models for market microstructure. Because the generative process of real markets is unknown, these models can be judged only by the realism of their output, leaving open what they actually learn. We make the question answerable by inverting the setup: we train GPT-style models on order flow from simulators whose latent parameters are known, so that the Bayes-optimal predictor, the generating state, and the exact data entropy serve as references. Measured against them, next-token pretraining on order flow implements amortized Bayesian inference over market regimes. The learned predictive matches the exact Bayes optimum to within a millinat per token and approaches the oracle floor even on a nonstationary family whose latent state drifts within an episode, a regime that requires filtering. The latent regime is linearly decodable from frozen activations, well above baselines, so a frozen-model read-out calibrates simulator parameters more accurately than windowed maximum likelihood at a fraction of the cost. In our experiments these capabilities are not produced by scale: capacity and token volume saturate early, and it is the diversity of training regimes, not the token count, that determines generalization to unseen markets. On real NASDAQ data, zero-shot transfer is set by how well the simulator prior covers real flow: an enriched mixture nearly closes the gap to an in-domain baseline, and pretraining beats from-scratch training at every budget across held-out tickers. Known ground truth turns average next-token loss, which is uninformative near the entropy floor, into an exact comparison against the Bayes-optimal and oracle predictors.

Video-HopChain: Multi-Hop Questions and Confidence-Gated Exploration for Video Reasoning Models

Abstract
HopChain has shown on still images that multi-hop data synthesis improves vision-language reasoning, because long chain-of-thought reasoning exposes errors that compound across steps, while most data used for reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) rarely demands a chain of visual evidence, so these weaknesses are likely to stay unexposed. We observe the same problem in video, where this framework has not yet been explored. We therefore build Video-HopChain, a dataset of 22,550 multi-hop video questions over 13,378 videos, together with a held-out benchmark of 1,000 questions. Each question chains three to six yes/no questions about moments in one video, and each yields one of two integers depending on its answer. The final answer is the sum of these integers, so an exact match on that sum gives the verifiable reward that RLVR needs. We first train Qwen3-VL-8B with GRPO on a standard video dataset, and a second stage on Video-HopChain then raises the mean over eight video understanding and reasoning benchmarks from 55.4 to 57.9 and improves every one of them. Training on such a dataset, however, exposes a known limitation of GRPO: its learning signal comes from the reward variance within a group, so hard questions whose rollouts are all incorrect and easy questions whose rollouts are all correct both leave the group with no gradient. To recover these groups at the same compute budget, we introduce Confidence-Gated Exploration (CGE). With 8 rollouts per question, CGE samples the first 4 as usual. If these 4 are either all correct or all incorrect, it samples the last 4 with the policy's most confident token masked inside the reasoning span, and removes the masked positions from the loss while all 8 rollouts enter the advantage. With CGE, the mean rises further to 59.3. We release the dataset, the checkpoint, and the data generation and training code.

Abstract
Recently, the integration of generative priors has revolutionized Monocular Depth Estimation (MDE), achieving state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance with remarkably high data efficiency. However, although methods based on Text-to-Image (T2I) models excel in general scenes, their efficacy degrades sharply when confronted with open-world environmental perturbations, such as illumination variations and adverse weather. Since T2I priors are optimized for semantic synthesis rather than preserving pixel-level structure, they struggle to decouple geometry from appearance under severe visual shifts, easily triggering structural hallucinations. Therefore, our core insight is to model open-world depth estimation as consistent geometric inference across multi-style images, fully leveraging the powerful structure-preserving editing priors of Image-to-Image (I2I) models. Additionally, we introduce a joint RGB latent reconstruction task, requiring the model to recover clean RGB information from degraded inputs. This multi-stage strategy effectively decouples environmental style interference, compelling the model to focus strictly on underlying geometric recovery. Extensive evaluations show that OpenDepth sets new SOTA records for robust zero-shot depth estimation across standard and extreme weather benchmarks, demonstrating exceptional structural integrity and adaptability.

Abstract
We present FoveaVLA, a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model that replaces the wrist camera with learned foveation actions. Wrist cameras provide close-up detail but restrict the view to the end-effector's surroundings. External cameras see the whole scene, but the policy receives them at low resolution, so the fine detail needed for manipulation is lost. FoveaVLA recovers this detail by predicting, alongside each action chunk, the interaction region to observe at the next policy query. The policy then receives crops of these regions, taken from the original-resolution images, together with the full views. We build FoveaVLA on $π_{0.5}$ in two configurations, Single and Dual, using one or two external stereo cameras with full and foveal RGB and depth inputs, and post-train both on DROID with RoboInter interaction annotations. Across four real-world task suites, FoveaVLA-Single achieves 70.8% success with one external RGB-D view, and FoveaVLA-Dual achieves 77.1%, versus 52.1% for the wrist-equipped $π_{0.5}$. Both match the baseline on plain pick-and-place and improve success with distractors, cloth manipulation, and far-away objects.
