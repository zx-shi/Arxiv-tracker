A Bayesian Optimized Biquad Filter-Burg Model for Real-Time EEG Phase Estimation
Authors: Ivan Martinez-Kay, Tatiana Kardashina, ShiNung Ching, Eric C. Leuthardt, Benjamin T. Acland
First: 2026-11-03T18:59:45+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T18:59:45+00:00
Comments: 24 pages, 12 figures (9 main, 3 supplements)

Abstract
Real-time phase estimation on stimulus-aligned transient EEG is critical for closed-loop neuromodulation systems targeting the theta-band, yet most real-time phase estimation methods have been developed and validated on resting-state, high-fidelity signals in the alpha band. Recent work has pursued deep learning architectures, while individualized classical bandpass filter tuning remains underexplored. This study characterizes the spectral and temporal properties of non-anticipative bandpass filtering and demonstrates the performance gains achieved through individualized tuning. We present a Bayesian-Optimized Biquad Filter-Burg (BOB) model evaluated on EEG recorded during peripheral vibrotactile stimulation of the left median nerve (theta, N=20 healthy subjects) and on resting-state recordings from the Max Planck Institute's LEMON dataset (alpha, N=20 healthy subjects). BOB was benchmarked against the Deep Dual-Branch Complex Neural Network (DBCNN), EEG Phase Prediction Network (EPN), Phastimate, and Phase Estimation with Autoregressive Padding (PEAP) under matched testing conditions: channel selection, individualized optimization, evaluation time points, and an offline reference phase obtained through zero-phase filtering and the Hilbert transform. Under these testing conditions, BOB achieved matched or better performance than these models at a fraction of the calibration time.

Agnostic Learning by Imitating
Authors: Sílvia Casacuberta, Parikshit Gopalan, Varun Kanade, Omer Reingold, Konstantinos Stavropoulos, Pranay Tankala
First: 2026-11-03T18:45:38+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T18:45:38+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Given a class of machine learning models and sample access to a data distribution with binary labels, which computational task is harder: training a discriminative model that distinguishes positive and negative labels as well as the best member of the class, or training a generative model whose outputs are indistinguishable from real data by all members of the class? We prove that these two fundamental tasks in computational learning theory are not just closely related, but actually equivalent to each other in complexity. One direction of this equivalence was previously known. Indeed, the powerful regularity lemma in complexity theory gives an efficient oracle reduction from the indistinguishability task (a.k.a. multiaccuracy) to the distinguishing task (a.k.a. agnostic learning) in the style of boosting algorithms. Our main contribution is an efficient oracle reduction in the other direction. Taking this idea further, we show that a multiaccuracy oracle is a remarkably powerful primitive, achieving not just agnostic learning but also a wide range of seemingly more demanding learning objectives via similarly efficient and direct constructions. Our results crucially use the power of making adaptive queries to the oracle, and stand in stark contrast to prior work, which showed that post-processing a single multiaccurate predictor is insufficient for even weak forms of learning. On a technical level, we draw a connection between our (offline) problem and a version of no-regret learning in the online setting, which allows us to use follow-the-leader type algorithms to modify the label distribution and aggregate the predictions of the multiaccuracy oracle.

Abstract
Recently, sparse autoencoders (SAEs) have emerged as an attractive tool for interpreting and interacting with representations in practical neural networks. While it is common empirical folklore, we also show theoretically that SAEs are highly unstable: different training runs are likely to produce different concept dictionaries and sparse codes. We characterize the model properties that hinder the stability of real-world SAEs, and address each of these problems through minimal changes to the architecture and training procedure. Together, these changes yield two versions of an \textbf{i}dentifiable SAE (iSAE), a variant of the standard TopK SAE with lower reconstruction error and improved stability. We explain this improvement theoretically by connecting SAEs with traditional dictionary learning approaches, and show that the dictionaries learned in practice satisfy an approximate restricted isometry condition, rendering the corresponding sparse codes in those models near-identifiable.

Left-Digit Bias: Theory and Empirical Evidence from a Tax Salience Experiment

Abstract
Demand often drops sharply at whole-dollar prices. To explain this phenomenon, known as left-digit bias, we develop a random utility model in which consumers consider a good only if its price is below a heterogeneous attention threshold. Masses of consumers with integer attention thresholds generate demand discontinuities. When a tax is introduced, the model predicts a weaker demand response compared to rational choice for left-digit biased consumers. Using data from a tax experiment, we find that the model matches behavioral moments and partially identify the distribution of willingness to pay relative to thresholds. A weak demand response need not imply a low excess burden of taxation and understanding the specific mechanism is important for welfare analysis.

Abstract
Machine learning models in radiotherapy are commissioned by testing them on cases with known answers, which may not reveal a shortcut that the test cases share with the training data. Frontier language models have recently been reported to read and write the raw weights of small neural networks, an ability called weight-space programming. The purpose of this study was to determine whether such a model, given only a clinical protocol and a network's weights, can audit and repair the network. Networks with 433 to 4,801 parameters were trained to screen head-and-neck planning requests against four rules. Flaws were planted through the training data: confounds that hid each rule, a 3 Gy shift in the cord threshold, and a hidden trigger. Claude Opus 5.5 audited each network blind, without tools, data or code execution, and every claim and repair was tested on about 420,000 requests. The language model named the broken rule in 16 of 18 flawed networks (95% CI 67% to 97%), including all 15 with a confound or a threshold shift. It repaired each of the 16 by changing a median of 3 parameters. Fine-tuning on protocol-labeled requests also repaired every network, but changed nearly every parameter. Each confound and shift it found was visible in single units, and a simple statistic chosen for each flaw type found the same flaws. It cleared 2 of 5 healthy networks without being forced and found 1 of 3 triggers. Testing against the protocol detected every flaw, and black-box testing found the confounds and the shift faster. The results indicate that, in small networks, weight-space programming can locate and repair flaws that silence an input or shift a threshold, but cannot yet certify that a network is correct.

Uniform local smoothing for the derivative NLS and the soliton mass threshold
Authors: Mohamed Majdoub
First: 2026-11-03T18:33:31+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T18:33:31+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Harrop-Griffiths, Killip, Ntekoume and Vişan proved that the derivative nonlinear Schrödinger equation (DNLS) is globally well-posed in $L^2(\mathbb{R})$. A key ingredient of their proof is a local smoothing estimate. It holds on $L^2$-equicontinuous sets of initial data, but it cannot hold on $L^2$-balls, because rescaled stationary solitons, all of mass $2π$, violate it. This raises a natural question: for which masses $m$ does local smoothing hold uniformly on the ball $\{M(q_0)\le m\}$? We prove three results. First, we give a quantitative version of the local smoothing estimate in which the data enter only through the mass and an explicit frequency scale $κ(q_0)$. On time intervals of length $2T$ the bound grows like $1+κ(q_0)^2T$, and the stationary soliton shows that the power of $κ$ cannot be lowered below one. Second, we prove uniform local smoothing on balls of small mass, together with a scale-invariant Morrey-type form and a localized $L^4_{t,x}$ bound. Third, we analyse the soliton family from both sides. The uniform constant must blow up at least like $(2π-m)^{-1}$ as $m\uparrow 2π$, while every soliton of mass $m<2π$ satisfies local smoothing with a constant $O((2π-m)^{-1})$. These facts single out the stationary-soliton mass $2π$, which is also the Hayashi--Ozawa coercivity threshold, as the natural candidate for the sharp threshold. We formulate this as a conjecture, give a spectral reformulation in terms of the Kaup--Newell scattering data, and discuss the obstacles to a proof.

Abstract
We study context-aware time-series forecasting, in which a single target variable is predicted from its own history and a set of contextual variables, such as weather and calendar information. As the context becomes more diverse, a single model struggles to capture all of its regimes, while ensemble learning and Mixture-of-Experts approaches rely on manual data partitioning or soft routing, which increases the inference cost and hinders interpretability. We propose CATF, a Context-Aware Time-Series Forecasting framework built on a Manager-Worker architecture: a Transformer-based Manager routes each input to a single Worker based on the context, using hard routing during both training and inference. The Workers thus specialize without manual data partitioning, and any single-model forecaster can serve as a Worker. On five context-rich datasets, four horizons, and two normalization schemes, CATF improves over its standalone counterpart in 162 of 200 comparisons, the best CATF-enhanced model outperforms the best standalone baseline in 19 of 20 settings, and it outperforms the zero-shot Time-MoE foundation model in 15 of 20 settings. A case study on bike-sharing data shows that the Manager routes inputs by the calendar and that each Worker specializes in a different weekly pattern.

Quantitative non-Gaussian and Gaussian approximation for exponential random graphs in the single-phase critical regime
Authors: Yi-Kun Zhao
First: 2026-11-03T18:24:07+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T18:24:07+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
We establish quantitative non-Gaussian and Gaussian approximations for edge-count fluctuations in dense exponential random graphs at single-phase criticality of any fixed even order $2m_*\ge 4$. The Hamiltonians use injective homomorphism counts of fixed simple graphs, with nonnegative leading coefficients for the higher-order terms. In bounded calibrated windows, edge-density fluctuations on the scale $n^{-1/m_*}$ admit an explicit non-Gaussian approximation with error $O(n^{-1/m_*})$. For parameter perturbations by fixed multiples of $n^{-1}$ with a nonzero first-order drift correction at the critical density, the deterministically centered edge count admits a Gaussian approximation on the scale $n^{(3m_*-2)/(2m_*-1)}$, with error $O(n^{-1/(2m_*-1)})$. Both rates hold in Wasserstein and Kolmogorov distances. The key step is to construct auxiliary kernels for exterior graphs outside an event of exponentially small probability. These kernels contract uniformly on the full row state space and agree with the conditional Gibbs updates on typical row configurations, yielding the conditional cumulant estimates used in Stein's method.

Abstract
Information Retrieval-based Bug Localization (IRBL) leverages bug reports to identify faulty source files. Evaluations of IRBL approaches primarily rely on labeled issues from issue tracking systems. However, prior research has shown that issue labels are frequently incorrect in open-source projects, threatening the validity of IRBL evaluations. Existing studies have primarily examined traditional IRBL techniques on small datasets, overlooking modern approaches and the partial overlap between noisy and cleaned datasets. In this paper, we conduct a large-scale empirical study to investigate the prevalence and impact of mislabeled issues in IRBL benchmarks. We analyze five benchmark datasets comprising over 55,000 issue reports and find that, on average, 34.82% of issues are mislabeled. We further evaluate six IRBL techniques, spanning traditional, deep learning-based, and large language model-based approaches using the widely adopted Ye et al. benchmark and statistical methods that account for dataset overlap. Our results show that removing mislabeled issues from evaluation datasets significantly reduces measured performance across all six techniques, with Mean Reciprocal Rank decreasing by 4.68%-21.74%. Although mislabeling does not change the relative ranking of techniques, it affects the magnitude of their performance differences. These findings highlight the importance of reliable evaluation data for ensuring valid and meaningful comparisons of IRBL techniques.

Abstract

Universal Hardness From Masking Channels
Authors: Parikshit Gopalan, Manoj Prabhakaran, Pranay Tankala
First: 2026-11-03T18:08:38+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T18:08:38+00:00

Abstract
We prove a broad learning-theoretic generalization of the celebrated hardcore lemma concerning the average-case complexity of computing a Boolean function (Impagliazzo, 1995). Our starting point is the observation that the classic notion of a hardcore measure, which reweights a function's input distribution, is equivalent to an input-dependent masking channel that injects randomness into the function's output distribution until it appears fully pseudorandom. The analogue of density of the measure is the fidelity of the channel. From this viewpoint, we develop a general framework for certifying hardness that allows for non-Boolean functions $f$ and a wide range of notions of hardness besides the classic 0-1 loss, by choosing the right notion of fidelity via convex duality. We show the difficulty of learning $f$ using a class $\mathcal{C}$ to minimize a convex loss is tightly characterized by the maximum fidelity possible for a masking channel, which measures the least amount of noise the channel must add to hide the structure of $f$ and make it $\mathcal{C}$-indistinguishable from random noise. Using our framework, we derive a number of multiclass generalizations of the hardcore lemma, and reveal some unexpected subtleties in going to larger alphabets. We construct a universal masking channel that applies to many natural notions of hardness simultaneously. Such channels are dual to omnipredictors, which optimize multiple losses at once (Gopalan, Kalai, Reingold, Sharan, and Wieder, 2022). We observe a symmetry between masking channels and generalizations of the hardcore lemma using pseudoentropy. We present a tight characterization of pseudoentropy using the MaxEnt algorithm from machine learning. A long line of research has shown that learning and hardness are two sides of the same coin; our framework gives a crisp and general formulation of this principle via convex duality.

Abstract
We attempt to alleviate failure modes of classical physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) in scenarios of topological pathologies corresponding to punctures, holes, poles, and voids by introducing de Rham cohomological augmentations, or Hodge-de Rham PINNs. Vanilla PINNs often encounter pitfalls in hole-based settings due to discontinuous interfaces, nonzero closed path integration, path dependence, correspondence to exact forms via the exterior derivative, etc., therefore we absolve traditional failures via parameterizing a 1-form by the Hodge-Morrey-Friedrichs (HMF) decomposition for manifolds with boundary, drawing a connection to de Rham cohomology such as through the HMF isomorphism theorem. 1-forms can act as representations of scalar-valued PDE solutions through integral operator mappings. We propose performing a PINN-based loss on the parameterized 1-form through an associated PDE, which subsequently solves the potential-based PINN problem and moreover remedies the failures of vanilla PINNs via the Sobolev-regular operator under a Fréchet differentiation interconnection. The operator relationship often has a nontrivial closed-form and often requires integration or a case-by-case analysis, which we address thoroughly in our experiments. When an integrability condition is not met on our losses, we can equip exhaustion functions to train the networks. We demonstrate our methods on the Aharonov-Bohm effect, which, to our understanding, is largely nonexistent in PINN literature. We develop theoretical guarantees of our methods and demonstrate our pathological safeguards on a broad selection of topologically-adverse problems, including scenarios of inexact forms, and those of nontrivial harmonics and coexact terms.

Competition for Survival and the Law of Maximum Entropy Production
Authors: Albert Han, Jiri Kataman-Kustwan, Alexey Bezryadin
First: 2026-05-25T16:47:34+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T17:56:59+00:00
Comments: 15 pages; 14 figures
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
The law of maximum entropy production (LMEP) is a selection principle stating that a non-equilibrium system will select the path or assembly of paths out of available paths that minimizes the potential or maximizes the entropy at the fastest rate given the constraints. Precise quantitative experimental tests are desirable for LMEP verification. Here we report electrical-circuit experiments on suspensions of silver particles which self-organize under electric field, form conducting paths and thus generate entropy by Joule heating. Our experiments on parallel-connected pairs of samples demonstrate a competitive relationship, thereby supporting the selection of the most efficient path. We find: (1) With sufficient voltage, a conducting bundle of silver particles forms, which acts as a self-organized dissipative structure (SODS). (2) With two samples in parallel, usually only one can develop SODS, due to the competition effect. As one sample approaches the maximum EPR, the others EPR is near zero. This confirms the path selection principle: only the highest EPR path survives. (3) At the end of the evolution, the sum of the sample EPR and the power supply circuit EPR approaches the calculated maximum. The deviation from the maximum is slightly lower when there are two competing samples. We identify the selection mechanism enabling natural selection and LMEP: A SODS gains a form of physical "fitness" when it establishes a pathway that efficiently suppresses the driving thermodynamic potential. As it develops and approaches the maximum EPR, the SODS modifies its nonequilibrium environment by consuming available free energy and eliminating the gradients that could support alternative pathways. We also examine the global implications of LMEP and propose that it serves as a driving mechanism propelling the hypothetical ascent of civilizations along the Kardashev scale.

Abstract
Given the full truth table of a Boolean function whose input is split between two players, we study the complexity of determining the minimum worst-case number of bits they must communicate to compute it. This is a basic meta-complexity question, posed by Yao in 1979. We prove NP-hardness of approximation within every fixed additive error for deterministic communication complexity, under deterministic polynomial-time reductions. The result holds both in the standard model and in Yao's original model, where the players alternate after every bit and only the receiver of the last bit must determine the answer. In particular, it resolves the exact computation question under Yao's original conventions. Earlier versions of this same paper established exact NP-hardness for the standard model, independently and concurrently with Hirahara, Ilango, and Loff. In both models, a polynomial-time estimator with any fixed additive error would yield a polynomial-time $3$-SAT algorithm, deterministic or bounded-error randomized according to the estimator. For the standard model, we also give quantitative implications for errors that grow with the number $T$ of table entries. A bounded-error randomized polynomial-time estimator with additive error $c\log T/\log\log T$, for any fixed $0\le c<1/8$, yields a bounded-error randomized $3$-SAT algorithm on $N$ variables and $O(N)$ clauses running in time $2^{O(N^α)}$ for every fixed $8c<α<1$. A deterministic polynomial-time estimator with additive error $c_*\log\log T$, for an effective absolute constant $c_*>0$, yields a deterministic algorithm running in time $2^{O((\log N)^2)}$ on such formulas.

The sum-of-squares complexity of linear-algebraic principles
Authors: Dmitriy Kunisky, Xifan Yu
First: 2026-11-03T17:35:56+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T17:35:56+00:00
Comments: 41 pages
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
We study the degree needed for sum-of-squares (SoS) proofs of several basic principles of real linear algebra. For any $n\times n$ matrix $A$ of rank greater than $r$, the Weak Rank Principle states that the equations $XY^\top=A$ with $n\times r$ matrix variables $X, Y$ have no solution. We show that this principle requires degree exactly $2r+2$ to prove. On the other hand, for $A=I_n$, setting $X = Y$ gives the Tight Frame Principle, which has a degree-$4$ proof. For $n\times n$ matrices, proving that a right inverse is also a left inverse requires degree exactly $2n+2$, while proving that orthonormal rows imply orthonormal columns again has a degree-4 proof. Using our lower bounds, we also derive related SoS degree lower bounds for polynomial systems encoding sign rank, matrix rigidity, small-width homogeneous non-commutative algebraic branching programs, and tensor CP rank. Lastly, we show that quantitative bounds on the Frobenius norms of the factors in the Weak Rank Principle can lower the degree required to refute $XY^\top=I_n$, but sufficiently large bounds still preserve the exact degree $2r+2$ from the unbounded case, giving a quantitative version of the Archimedean property arising in classical results on SoS proofs.

Abstract
Implicit generative models based on deep neural networks have revolutionized many scientific fields, from weather research to language generation. They successfully model complex random objects arising in real-world problems. In this work, we propose a sample-efficient training mechanism for these models. Our approach leverages regularized inference with shrinkage priors and generalized Bayes. In the large $p$ (parameter dimension) and small $n$ (sample size) setting, sparsity-inducing priors have been successfully used for linear regression when $p \gg n$. Abstract
